# Literature position and interpretation

## 1. Established foundations

I use regression-based stopping policies and martingale dual evaluation to study exercise accuracy under a funded cash-event model. These methodological foundations are established. [Longstaff and Schwartz (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/14.1.113) provide the continuation-regression method, and [Haugh and Kogan (2004)](https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.1030.0070) provide the approximate-value dual construction. The distinction between a feasible policy value and an upper assessment of the optimum also predates this project. [Andersen and Broadie (2004)](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1040.0258) develop a primal-dual simulation procedure for pricing intervals.

Non-nested dual evaluation is not a new contribution of this repository. [Belomestny, Bender and Schoenmakers (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9965.2008.00357.x) construct estimators that preserve the martingale property. [Cline (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1080/14697688.2026.2647986) studies regression-based dual algorithms and states conditional restrictions on their projection residuals. These papers prevent attributing novelty to computational efficiency or approximate-value martingales alone. I have not implemented their complete algorithms as comparative benchmarks here.

## 2. The question addressed by this project

I distinguish three interventions: changing a stopping policy, changing its evaluation martingale, and changing its exercise opportunities. Each identifies a different quantity. Replacing a valid martingale while retaining the policy can reduce dual slack without increasing the reward earned by the policy. A common martingale permits paired differences to identify policy gains. Changing the calendar requires stating whether the original rule, its terminal decision or its earlier training target also changes.

This organisation makes a claim about computational allocation testable. Spending more on evaluation can improve knowledge about an unchanged policy; spending more on training can change its reward. A smaller dual gap alone cannot establish which expenditure creates more economic value. Identifying that trade-off requires matched financial inputs, declared workloads and independent evaluation. The [completed paper](README.md) reports conditional simulation comparisons of loss assessment, terminal-policy adjustment, repeated-training precision and CPU prediction. The retained comparisons support improved estimated precision under the studied specifications. They do not establish a universally optimal allocation, superiority at exactly equal realised cost or independent market validity. Failed cost forecasts remain part of the findings.

## 3. Why the conditional-drift derivation is included

The derivation in Appendix A addresses the consequences of replacing exact conditional centring with an approximation. Its starting point is the predictable compensator: subtracting accumulated conditional increment means from an adapted penalty produces a martingale. Optional sampling then converts the residual drift into an explicit allowance for the policy-loss bound. The same calculation gives the bias remaining in a paired comparison after the common upper payoff cancels.

This is an application of established theory. I do not describe the result, proof or finite counterexample as novel. Independent exposition establishes neither priority nor the absence of an earlier derivation. The purpose is to make the consequences of the assumptions transparent within this research design. The counterexample shows why unconditional mean-zero increments and non-negative gaps cannot certify the conditional requirement. It is not a replication or a rejection of Cline's algorithms.

The rational-arithmetic implementation checks the inequalities and paired identity against finite-tree stopping policies and a separate dynamic programme. Every input is a declared mathematical fixture. No simulated observation is represented as a market observation, and none of these checks establishes an empirical result or a floating-point error certificate.

For Cline, I consulted the published version, principally Sections 3.3.1, 4.1 and 4.2, including Conditions A and B and the discussion of conditional approximation error. The comparison concerns constructions and assumptions; it does not reproduce the papers' numerical tables.

This focused review is sufficient to attribute the methods used here. It is not an exhaustive priority search. A claim of a new theoretical contribution would require a separate comparison of precise assumptions and conclusions, including related results outside option pricing. I retain the source versions and reading scope locally; the repository provides bibliographic references without redistributing the articles.

## 5. Training information and computational allocation

The 30 September revision distinguishes evaluation of a realised policy from estimation of the mean value of a fixed training procedure. I derive the repeated-training variance decomposition by conditioning on a fitted pair, applying total variance and averaging independent pairs. An explicit product probability space permits preceding evidence to determine fresh sample counts while keeping the training and evaluation laws fixed. Initial enlargement of the evaluation filtration makes the fitted coefficients part of the stopping rule's information and justifies conditional optional sampling. A prescribed joint training law allows the two fits within a pair to share paths; it does not impose independence on a comparison intended to be paired.

These arguments apply established probability methods to the research design. The continuous allocation formula follows by substituting an affine cost constraint into the variance and differentiating; it is an illustrative calculation, not an empirical optimum or a CPU guarantee. The finite cost-stopping example identifies a limitation of the fixed-count proof. It shows that an unbiased stopped sum need not remain unbiased after division by its random count. I do not describe any of these derivations as a new theorem or claim priority for the example.

[Glynn and Whitt (1992)](https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.40.3.505), *Operations Research*, 40(3), 505–520, provide the published context for treating stochastic computational cost jointly with simulation error. The retained source is a scan of the published article obtained from the coauthor's Columbia webpage. I consulted journal pages 505–507 and checked the author abstract and bibliographic details through Safari. I did not consult the later random-time-change proof for this addition and do not cite it as support for the finite example. The regression and approximate-value constructions remain attributed to Longstaff and Schwartz (2001) and Haugh and Kogan (2004). These probability derivations introduce no fitted market quantities; the paper reports the numerical allocation and CPU findings separately.

## 6. Ratio inference and allocation decisions

The component-ratio confidence set applies established test inversion. [Fieller (1954)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2517-6161.1954.tb00159.x), *Journal of the Royal Statistical Society, Series B*, 16(2), 175–185, supplies the ratio-contrast and quadratic-inversion foundation. I consulted the publisher abstract and extracted text from journal pages 175–177, including equations (5)–(10). The attempted publisher PDF access did not yield a retained local file or visual inspection. This is a limited textual consultation, not a complete reading of the article.

I also consulted [von Luxburg and Franz (2009)](https://www3.stat.sinica.edu.tw/sstest/j19n3/j19n312/j19n312.html), *Statistica Sinica*, 19(3), 1095–1117, in the final published PDF. The consulted text covers pages 1095–1108, with pages 1102–1103 inspected visually. Equation (2.1), Construction 2 and Theorem 5 motivate inversion of linear-contrast tests and explain why the ratio set inherits the coverage of those tests. The treatment of unbounded sets explains why a weakly identified denominator must not be replaced silently by a bounded interval.

In Appendix C, I derive the component estimates from the joint moments of paired training means and their conditional evaluation variances. I retain the full sampling covariance and use asymptotic normal contrasts. This application does not inherit exact normal-model Fieller coverage. It requires independent repetitions, the relevant finite moments, a differentiable moment map and non-degenerate limiting contrasts; small-sample accuracy remains unestablished. Exact arithmetic for the resulting quadratic cannot repair an inaccurate distributional approximation.

The subsequent endpoint comparison follows by differentiating the relative-variance expression with respect to the component ratio. I include the derivation because it makes the allocation rule reproducible and states precisely which uncertainty set it protects against. The fourth-moment standard-error calculation similarly applies the delta method to a variance contrast. These are independently written applications to the experiment, without a claim of theoretical priority. Their assumptions and numerical interpretation are stated in the [paper source](main.tex); the [bibliographic file](references.bib) records the published references.
